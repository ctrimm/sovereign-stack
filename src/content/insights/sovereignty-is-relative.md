Ask ten vendors what "sovereign" means and you'll get ten marketing answers. Here's the working definition this directory uses: **a product is sovereign relative to a buyer when no unwanted third party — foreign government, acquirer, or adversary — can reach the buyer's data through the vendor.**

Note what that definition does not say. It doesn't say European. It doesn't say open source. It says: relative to *you*.

## Domicile vs. residency

Two fields on every listing in this directory, and the two most confused concepts in the industry:

- **Domicile** — where the company is legally registered. This determines which government's courts can compel the company to act.
- **Residency** — where customer data physically rests. This determines which government's police can walk into a datacenter.

Residency without matching domicile is the industry's favorite illusion. Data in Frankfurt owned by a Delaware corporation is governed in practice by US law — the CLOUD Act explicitly reaches data held by US companies regardless of where the servers sit. Conversely, a Swiss company hosting in Frankfurt is an odd but coherent choice: Swiss corporate law, German physical security.

When the two match — German company, German datacenters — you get the strongest, simplest story. That's why this directory lets you filter on both.

## The three legal regimes that matter

**The US CLOUD Act.** US-domiciled providers must hand over data in their "possession, custody, or control" regardless of where it's stored. This is the single fact that makes domicile a first-class field. It doesn't make US vendors unusable — it makes them a *deliberate choice* rather than a default.

**GDPR and Schrems II.** EU law restricts transfers of personal data to countries without adequate protection. Schrems II killed the Privacy Shield; the current Data Privacy Framework survives until it doesn't. An EU-domiciled vendor hosting in the EU sidesteps the entire transfer regime.

**Sectoral regimes.** Switzerland's nFADP, Canada's PIPEDA, the UK's GDPR-alike — each adequate or not in the EU's eyes, each with its own compulsion rules. The directory records domicile precisely so you can do this homework per vendor instead of trusting a badge.

## How to actually choose

1. **Name your adversary.** A competitor? A foreign intelligence service? A acquirer doing due diligence? Different threats, different layers.
2. **Match domicile to your own legal reality.** An EU company usually wants EU domicile; a Brazilian company might prefer Brazilian or EU over US.
3. **Treat "EU-hosted" as a claim to verify**, not a conclusion. Check the domicile field on the listing.
4. **Prefer open source where the stakes are high**, because legal jurisdiction is about compelling the vendor — and a vendor that can't read your data (end-to-end encryption, self-hosting) has nothing to hand over.

Sovereignty isn't a product you buy. It's a set of decisions you make, layer by layer, with your eyes open. The directory exists to make those decisions informed ones.
