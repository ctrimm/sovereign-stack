Nobody pays for Gmail. That's not because email is cheap to run — it's because you're not the customer. The sovereign tech conversation usually frames this morally. Let's frame it economically instead, because the economics explain why "free" keeps winning and what it actually costs.

## The three kinds of free

**Ad-supported free** (Gmail, Google Analytics, Facebook): you pay with behavioral data, which funds the service and then some. The cost is diffuse — a little privacy from billions of people — which is exactly why it's hard to feel and easy to ignore.

**Venture-subsidized free** (Notion's free tier, Vercel's hobby tier, every startup's free plan): you pay nothing today because investors are paying for your future conversion. The cost arrives later, as a price hike, a tier cut, or an acquisition. When the subsidy ends, migration is priced to hurt.

**Community free** (Nextcloud, Mastodon, Linux): you pay with nothing but attention, because the software is a commons. The cost is real but distributed — maintainers burn out, security review is underfunded — and it's the only kind of free with no trapdoor.

## The acquisition lottery

Here's the cost nobody models: every venture-backed tool you adopt is a lottery ticket where *you* are the prize. Figma was independent until Adobe offered $20 billion. Every "free forever" startup is for sale at the right price, and your data, workflows, and integrations transfer with the cap table. Sovereignty-conscious buyers should price this risk explicitly: what happens to your stack if this vendor is acquired by a company in an adverse jurisdiction next year?

Open source is the hedge. Not because open-source companies can't be acquired — they can — but because the code can't be un-published. When the vendor changes, the community can fork. That's not theory; it's the entire history of open-source infrastructure.

## Doing the real math

A 10-person team on Google Workspace + Slack + a few SaaS tools spends roughly $15,000–25,000/year. The sovereign equivalent — a Hetzner server, managed email from a European provider, self-hosted chat and files — typically runs $2,000–5,000/year. The "free" stack was never cheaper. It was just priced in a currency (your data, your lock-in, your acquisition risk) that doesn't appear on the invoice.

The directory exists partly to make the real math visible: every listing shows pricing, license, and domicile up front. Sovereign tech isn't a luxury purchase. In most cases, it's the cheaper one — once you count all the currencies.
